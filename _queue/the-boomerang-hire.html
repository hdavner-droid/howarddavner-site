The Boomerang Hire

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

An email showed up last month from someone who had been on my team years ago, before Provieo existed, back when I was still hiring for a much smaller operation. She wanted to know if there was a seat for her again. My first reaction was warmth, which surprised me a little, because the last conversation we had was an exit interview, and exit interviews are rarely warm. My second reaction, a few seconds later, was suspicion of my own warmth. I liked her. I remembered her work fondly. Neither of those things is evidence that hiring her back is the right decision.

The instinct to say yes to a boomerang hire is strong and mostly comes from the wrong place. It feels like closure. It feels like the company gets to reclaim something it lost. There is a story we tell ourselves where the person left, learned something out there in the world, and is coming back improved, like a returning traveler. Sometimes that story is true. But just as often the real story is simpler and less flattering: the market got tighter, the new job did not deliver what was promised, and the old job looks better now only by comparison to disappointment.

Why They Left in the First Place

The question I actually need answered is not whether I liked working with this person. It is whether the condition that made her leave still exists. People leave for reasons that are usually specific and often unspoken in the moment. Money. A manager. A ceiling they could see clearly from where they sat. Boredom with a role that stopped growing. If that condition is still true, rehiring her just delays the same ending by however long it takes to rediscover it. I have made this mistake once, rehiring someone into a nearly identical role to the one she left, telling myself things were different now, when the only thing different was that I missed her. She left again within a year, and I do not blame her for it. I blame myself for not asking the harder question before I made the offer.

What makes boomerang hiring worth taking seriously anyway, despite that failure, is that it removes the biggest source of uncertainty in any hire, which is not knowing how someone actually works. You already know how she handles a bad week. You know whether she tells you about a problem early or lets it become a crisis first. You know if she is honest when she is wrong. That is exactly the information Provieo tries to surface for hires who are strangers, through demonstrated work instead of a polished interview performance, and with a boomerang candidate you already have years of it, for free, without needing a platform to prove anything.

What Changed, Not Who They Are

So the diligence I try to do now is narrower than a normal interview and pointed in a different direction. I am not trying to learn who this person is, because I already know. I am trying to learn what changed, both in her and in the role. What did the two years away actually teach her, specifically, not in the general sense of growth but in terms of what she can now do that she could not do before. And on my end, is the actual thing that pushed her out the door fixed, or did I just forget about it because enough time passed that the memory softened. If I cannot answer that second question honestly, I have no business making the offer, no matter how much I liked working with her.

I ended up saying yes to the email, but not to the same job. The role I offered her sits one level up from where she left, with a mandate she never had before, built partly on things I watched her do after she left, work I could see because so much of it now lives in public somewhere, a portfolio rather than a rumor. It felt less like reopening an old chapter and more like starting a new one with someone whose first chapter I happened to have read already. That distinction, whether you are hiring the same person into the same hole or hiring a person who has actually moved since you last saw her, is the whole difference between a boomerang hire that works and one that is just nostalgia wearing an offer letter.

I still do not think there is a clean rule here, other than to be suspicious of your own affection long enough to ask the plain question you would ask a stranger. People change less than we hope and more than we expect, and figuring out which is true in any given case takes real effort, not a fond memory and a signature.
