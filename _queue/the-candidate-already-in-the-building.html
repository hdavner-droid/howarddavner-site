The Candidate Already In The Building

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

A few years ago at NERD Focus we spent six weeks searching for a plant operations manager. We used a recruiter, interviewed a dozen people, flew two of them in, and hired someone who lasted four months before he quit over a disagreement about scheduling. The person who eventually took the role had been running the night shift on our packaging line the entire time we were searching. Nobody put her name forward until the second search, because nobody thought to look at the person already in the building.

I have thought about that hire more than almost any other decision I made in those early years, not because it was catastrophic but because it was so unnecessary. The information we needed was sitting in front of us. She had already solved three separate production bottlenecks on her own initiative, had trained two other employees without being asked, and had a better read on our actual capacity constraints than any of the external candidates who talked confidently about operations frameworks. We just were not looking at her as a candidate. We were looking at her as staff.

The Bias Toward the Unknown

There is something almost superstitious about the way companies prefer external hires. An outside candidate arrives with a clean story, one you construct yourself out of a resume and a few hours of conversation. You get to imagine them into the role. An internal candidate arrives with a full history, including the times they were late, the project that went sideways, the meeting where they said something awkward. That history feels like a liability, but it is actually the opposite. It is the only real data you will ever have about how someone performs under your specific conditions, with your specific team, under your specific pressure. External hiring is a bet on a story. Internal hiring is a bet on a track record, and somehow we treat the track record as the riskier option.

Part of this is structural. Most companies do not have a formal way to surface internal candidates the way they have a formal process for external search. There is no job board that includes the people who already work there. A manager posts a role, a recruiter sources candidates, and the process runs entirely outside the walls of the company, as if talent were something that only exists elsewhere. I built Provieo around the idea that demonstrated work should replace credentials as the basis for hiring, and it took me longer than it should have to realize the most demonstrated work of all is usually sitting inside the organization doing it every day, unrecorded and unconsidered.

What the Internal Candidate Actually Offers

The strongest argument for looking inward is not sentimental. It is practical. An internal candidate already understands the constraints you are operating under, the personalities they will need to work with, and the actual problems the role exists to solve, as opposed to the idealized version of the problem written into the job description. They ramp faster because they are not learning the company and the job at the same time. They also tend to be more loyal in a way that has nothing to do with gratitude and everything to do with continuity. They know what it cost them to get there and they are less likely to treat the role as a stepping stone to somewhere else.

None of this means external hiring is wrong. Sometimes you need a perspective that does not exist inside your walls, someone who has seen a problem solved at a different scale or in a different industry. But that should be a deliberate choice, made after you have honestly assessed who is already capable, not a default you fall into because internal search feels harder to organize than posting a job externally and letting applications come in.

Building the Habit of Looking Inward

What changed for me after that hiring mistake was not a new process so much as a new question. Before opening any search, I started asking who on the team is already doing pieces of this work, even informally, and whether we had ever asked them directly if they wanted it. That single question surfaced two more strong internal hires over the following two years, people who would never have applied on their own because it did not occur to them that the door was open. The work of finding talent, I have come to believe, is less about casting a wider net and more about paying closer attention to the net you already have.
