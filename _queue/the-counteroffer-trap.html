The Counteroffer Trap

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

A few years ago a plant manager at Beverage USA Holdings walked into my office with a resignation letter he had clearly rewritten three or four times. He had a competing offer, about twelve percent more than he was making with us, and he was nervous handing it over. I did the thing most executives do in that moment. I asked him what it would take to stay, then went and got him a number that matched the offer plus a little more, because he was good and I did not want to lose him mid-quarter. He stayed. Six months later he left anyway, for a company that had not even recruited him this time. He just started looking again on his own.

I have thought about that sequence more than almost any other personnel decision I have made, because it looked like a win in the moment and turned out to be a delay. The raise did not fix anything. It just moved the resignation six months down the calendar and cost me the twelve percent in the meantime. What actually needed fixing was whatever had made him quiet enough, for long enough, that his first move was a job search rather than a conversation with me.

The Question Nobody Asks

The strange thing about a counteroffer is that it answers a question nobody bothered to ask before the resignation letter showed up. If a company can suddenly find twelve percent the day someone threatens to leave, that money existed the whole time. The real question is why it took a threat to surface it, and almost nobody sits with that question long enough to get an honest answer. The easier move is to celebrate the save, tell the team you fought to keep a good person, and go back to whatever you were doing before the letter interrupted your day.

What I have come to believe is that a counteroffer is rarely about money at all, even when money is the mechanism. It is about the fact that the person felt invisible enough, or undervalued enough, or bored enough, to spend their evenings interviewing somewhere else without telling you first. The raise treats the symptom. It does nothing about the condition that made them start looking, which usually has more to do with growth, recognition, or a manager they had stopped trusting than with the number on their paycheck.

What The Raise Actually Buys

There is also a quieter cost that shows up after the counteroffer is accepted. The person who stays knows, and their manager knows, that the relationship changed the day money had to be produced under duress. I have watched people who took counteroffers become slightly less trusted by the managers who gave them, not because anyone said so out loud, but because loyalty that had to be purchased reads differently than loyalty that was simply there. The person also knows they left a card on the table by not asking for more before they had another offer in hand, which is its own kind of resentment that tends to surface later, usually at review time.

None of this means you should refuse to counter an offer on principle. Sometimes the fix really is compensation that fell behind the market through simple neglect, and matching it is the right and honest move. The mistake is treating the counteroffer as the whole fix rather than the opening of a conversation that should have happened months earlier. The plant manager and I never had that conversation. I handled the crisis and skipped the diagnosis, and the second departure was really just the first one arriving late.

At Provieo I think about this constantly from the other side, because so much of what we build is designed to reduce the number of moments where a resignation letter is the first honest signal a manager receives. A track record of actual work, reviewed regularly rather than once a year, tends to surface dissatisfaction and stagnation long before someone updates their resume. I do not think any system removes the need for managers to pay attention to their own people, but I would rather find out someone is unhappy from a pattern in their work than from a letter they have already rewritten four times.
