The First Ninety Days Are a Promise

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

You can tell a great deal about a company by what happens to someone in their first ninety days. Not the swag or the welcome lunch — those are easy. I mean whether the work they were hired to do actually arrives, whether the person who hired them is still paying attention in week six, whether the picture painted in the interview survives contact with the job. Onboarding is where a company either keeps the promises it made to get someone in the door, or quietly breaks them and hopes the new hire is too polite to say so.

I've broken that promise before, without meaning to. You hire someone in a burst of conviction, and then the fire that made you hire them moves to the next fire, and the new person spends their first month waiting for something real to do. They don't complain. They just recalibrate — downward — what this place is going to be. By the time you look up, the person who showed up eager has learned to expect less, and that lesson is expensive to unteach.

Real work is the only real welcome

The best thing you can give a new hire early is a piece of real work with their name on it — something small enough to be safe and consequential enough to matter. Not a shadowing rotation, not a reading list, not a month of meetings. Ownership, quickly. People decide whether they're trusted here long before you think the evaluation has begun, and nothing says "we trust you" like handing over something that's actually yours to get right or wrong.

Onboarding is a two-way audition

We tend to frame the first ninety days as the company evaluating the hire. It's at least as much the hire evaluating the company. They're deciding whether we are who we said we were, whether the mission in the job post shows up in how Tuesdays actually go, whether the person they'll report to is someone worth doing hard things for. If you treat that window as a formality, the good ones notice, and the good ones have options.

So I've started thinking of onboarding less as a process and more as a promise coming due. Everything we implied to get someone to say yes — the autonomy, the mission, the standard of the people around them — gets tested in those first weeks, and the test is pass-fail in the new hire's private ledger. Get it right and you've bought years of earned loyalty. Get it wrong and you'll spend those same years wondering why the person you were so sure about never quite caught fire.
