The Interview Nobody Prepares For

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

A few years ago I sat in on a final-round interview where the candidate had clearly done everything right. She had researched the company, her stories were tight, and her answers arrived in the order the textbooks suggest. Then the hiring manager asked a small, almost offhand question: what would you have done differently on the project you just described? There was a pause of maybe four seconds. What came next was the first unrehearsed thing she had said all hour, and it was the only part of the interview I remember in detail.

I have thought about that pause often since. Interview preparation has become an industry, and like most industries it optimizes for what can be measured, which here means polish. Candidates learn to anticipate the standard questions and deliver answers that sound like judgment. Interviewers, for their part, have learned to ask the standard questions because they are defensible and easy to compare. The result is two people performing a conversation that neither one believes is revealing much. We all know this and keep doing it, mostly because nobody has offered a comfortable alternative.

What the unplanned moment reveals

The reason the unplanned question works is not that it is clever. It works because it asks for something that cannot be retrieved from memory. A rehearsed story is a recording. A question about what you would change forces you to reopen the work, look at it again, and decide in real time what you actually think. You find out whether the person understands the causes of their own results or only the narrative that surrounds them. In my experience those are very different things, and the gap between them predicts a lot about how someone behaves when a project goes sideways at eleven at night.

I should admit that I was not always good at this. Early in my career in finance I interviewed people the way I had been interviewed, with a list and a scorecard. I hired two people in that period who interviewed beautifully and struggled for reasons I could have seen coming if I had pushed even once past the prepared answer. I do not think they misled me. I think I never asked them to do anything they had not already practiced, so I learned only how well they practiced.

Moving the conversation toward the work

What changed my approach was building a business where the cost of a bad hire showed up quickly and in plain view. At Beverage USA Holdings, and later in building Provieo, I started asking people to walk me through something they had actually made, and then I stopped following the script. I asked what they had thrown away, what they would not defend anymore, where the numbers surprised them. Someone who did the work answers these questions with a kind of specific, slightly reluctant detail. Someone who stood near the work tends to answer with generalities, and the difference becomes audible within a few minutes.

This is a large part of why I believe demonstrated work should carry more weight in hiring than it does. A piece of real work gives the interviewer something concrete to probe, which makes the conversation harder to fake and more useful to both sides. The candidate, meanwhile, gets to talk about something they know deeply rather than guess at what the interviewer wants to hear. I have seen nervous people become precise and generous the moment the subject shifted from themselves to a thing they built.

What candidates can do about it

If you are on the other side of the table, the practical advice is less glamorous than it sounds. Prepare less of your answers and more of your material. Know your own work well enough that you could be interrupted at any point and still explain why you made each decision, including the ones you now think were wrong. Be willing to say that you would change something. In my experience, admitting a flaw in your own work reads as confidence, because only people who understand the work can describe its weaknesses accurately.

I am not certain any interview format can fully escape performance, and I would be suspicious of anyone who claimed otherwise. People will always present their best selves, and that is not a defect. But the best interviews I have run were the ones where, somewhere in the middle, the script quietly gave out and both of us were simply thinking about a real problem. If you are hiring, aim for that moment on purpose. If you are being hired, do the work that makes it a moment you welcome rather than one you dread.
