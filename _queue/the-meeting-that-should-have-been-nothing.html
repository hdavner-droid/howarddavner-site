The Meeting That Should Have Been Nothing

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

A few years ago I sat through a weekly meeting, forty minutes long, where I realized that nobody in the room could say who had started it. There were seven of us. Each person gave an update that the others had already read in a shared document. Then we nodded, and the meeting ended. When I asked afterward why we held it, the answers were all some version of the same thing: it was on the calendar. That is the whole origin story of most recurring meetings. Someone had a real problem once, called a meeting to solve it, and the meeting outlived the problem by a long time.

I think this matters more than it appears to, because a recurring meeting is not a single cost. It is a standing decision that gets renewed automatically, every week, without anyone choosing to renew it. Nobody would approve a budget line that said seven people, forty minutes, indefinitely, for the purpose of reading things aloud. Yet that is what we had approved by never looking at it. My background in finance makes this feel familiar. The expenses that hurt a business are rarely the dramatic ones. They are the small subscriptions nobody cancels because cancelling requires someone to notice them first.

What a meeting is actually for

When I started questioning my own calendar, I tried to sort meetings by what they were actually doing. I found only a few honest purposes. Some meetings exist to make a decision that needs several people and real disagreement. Some exist to share information that is too nuanced for writing. Some exist so that people who work apart still feel like a team. And a large remainder exist to reduce the anxiety of whoever called them, a way of checking that things are on track without having to look at the work itself.

That last category was the uncomfortable one, because I was the person who had called several of them. A status meeting often says something about the manager rather than the project. I wanted reassurance, and I was spending other people's afternoons to get it. The fix was not complicated, but it was humbling: I started reading the work directly, and the need for the meeting mostly dissolved. I will admit I did not do this gracefully at first. I cancelled one meeting too quickly, a conversation that had been quietly doing the third job on my list, and for a month the team felt a little more distant from each other. I had cut something I had misclassified.

How I audit it now

Twice a year I go through my recurring meetings one at a time and ask a plain question: if this did not exist today, would I create it? If the answer is yes, I keep it and try to say out loud what it is for. If the answer is no, I cancel it for a month and watch what happens. Usually nothing happens, which is the information I needed. Occasionally someone comes to me and says they missed it, and then I know what it was really doing, and I can bring it back in a smaller or better form.

I also try to make the written alternative honest. If a meeting existed to share updates, the update has to live somewhere people will actually read it, and someone has to be responsible for asking questions in writing. This is where many replacements fail. A document nobody reads is just a meeting that has become invisible. At Provieo we care a great deal about demonstrated work being visible on its own terms, and I have come to believe the same applies internally. If the work is clear enough to be seen, you rarely need an hour of people describing it.

The cost nobody counts

What I notice most is not the hours saved but the quality of the hours that remain. When the calendar is full of standing obligations, people do their real thinking in the gaps, tired and in fragments. Clearing even a few of those obligations gives back long stretches that can be used for work requiring concentration. I cannot offer a number for this and I would distrust anyone who did. I can only say that the team makes better decisions when they are not arriving at them between calls.

I do not think the answer is to abolish meetings. Some of the most useful hours of my working life have been in a room with a few people arguing about something hard. The point is that a meeting should have to earn its place more than once. Put the question on your own calendar this week and see which ones you would not create again. You may find, as I did, that a surprising number of them were never really decided in the first place.
