The Promotion Nobody Asked For

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

A few years ago at Beverage USA Holdings, we had a regional account rep who was, by every number we tracked, the best in the company. He opened more doors, kept more shelf space, and had a way of making distributors feel like partners instead of vendors. So we did what companies do with their best people. We made him a regional manager. Eight months later he asked to go back to being an individual contributor, and by then we had already lost two of the reps who reported to him. The company had not gained a manager. It had lost a top performer and gained a mediocre one, plus some collateral damage.

I do not think this is a rare story. It is closer to the default outcome when you use performance in a role as a proxy for readiness in a different role that shares almost nothing with it except proximity on an org chart. Selling and managing salespeople draw on different instincts. One rewards obsession with your own pipeline. The other rewards patience with other people's pipelines, most of which will move slower and messier than yours ever did. Nobody sat him down and asked whether he wanted to trade the thing he was great at for a job he had never tried. We just assumed excellence was fungible, that it would simply relocate to wherever we pointed it.

The Real Question We Skipped

What we should have asked, and did not, was whether he had ever shown any evidence of wanting to make other people better at his job. Not whether he was well liked, which he was, and not whether he was ambitious, which he clearly was. Whether, unprompted, he had ever coached a newer rep through a bad quarter, or written up how he handled a difficult distributor so someone else could use it. Those are small, checkable behaviors, and they tend to show up long before a promotion decision if anyone bothers to look. We did not look. We looked at a leaderboard and made an inference that felt obvious at the time and looks careless in hindsight.

This is one of the things Provieo was built to fix in a smaller way, though the version of the problem we see is usually earlier in someone's career. A candidate applies for a role that requires collaboration or mentorship, and the only evidence a resume can offer is a job title and a tenure. The work itself, the actual artifacts of how someone operates, rarely makes it into the hiring conversation. We are trying to make that evidence visible before the offer goes out, not after, because after is expensive. It is expensive in salary, in the six months it takes to notice the fit is wrong, and in whatever damage gets done to the people now reporting to someone who was promoted for the wrong reasons.

What I Would Do Differently

If I were making that call again, I would separate the conversation about compensation from the conversation about role. Our rep did not need a new title to be paid like our best performer. We could have built a senior individual contributor track that recognized him without asking him to become someone else. Instead we conflated reward with promotion, which is a common and understandable mistake, because most companies only have one ladder and it goes through management. The fix is not complicated. It is just unusual, because building a second ladder takes more organizational discipline than pointing at the org chart and adding a box.

I am not certain we would have made the right call even with better process, because some of this only reveals itself once someone is actually managing people and discovers they hate it or love it. There is a limit to how much you can predict in advance, and I try not to pretend otherwise when I talk about this. But the limit is much narrower than we treated it. We had signals available to us and chose not to use them, mostly because asking felt slower than assuming.

He did eventually find his footing again as a rep, and he is still one of our best, which is the part of the story that makes me most forgiving of the mistake and least forgiving of how we made it. The cost was not permanent, but it was real, and it was entirely avoidable with about twenty minutes of a harder conversation before the promotion instead of a harder conversation after.
