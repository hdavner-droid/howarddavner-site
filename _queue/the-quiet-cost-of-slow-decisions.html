The Quiet Cost of Slow Decisions

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

Years ago, in my finance career, I watched a team wait three weeks to answer a vendor's simple question. Nobody was careless. Everyone was being thorough. A spreadsheet was revised twice, a senior person asked for one more comparison, and a meeting was moved because the right attendee was traveling. When the answer finally went out, it was the answer we would have given on day two. The vendor had moved on, and so, I noticed, had the junior analyst who built the original recommendation. He never again brought us something unprompted.

I have thought about that analyst often since I started building companies. The visible cost of a slow decision is the delay itself, and that is usually the smaller cost. The larger one is what the delay teaches. When people see good work sit in a queue, they draw a quiet conclusion about whether effort at the edges of their job is worth it. They do not announce this. They simply do what is asked, on time, and nothing more. Managers then wonder why their teams lack initiative, when the team has been taught, accurately, that initiative goes nowhere.

Why slowness feels like virtue

Slow decisions are comfortable to the person making them. Waiting for more information feels responsible, and nobody gets blamed for asking a good question. A decision made quickly and wrongly is attributed to you. A decision delayed until it no longer matters is attributed to circumstances. I have caught myself in this more than once. In the early days of Beverage USA Holdings, I sat on a supplier choice for longer than I should have, telling myself I wanted one more quote. In truth, I was uneasy about the commitment, and the quote was a way of not looking at that unease directly.

What I eventually learned to ask is what information would actually change my mind. If I cannot name it, I am not gathering data, I am postponing discomfort. That question is not a perfect filter, and there are decisions where waiting really does improve the answer. But it separates the two cases more often than I expected, and it is embarrassing how frequently the honest reply was nothing.

Sorting decisions by how hard they are to undo

The most useful habit I have adopted is sorting decisions by reversibility rather than importance. Some choices are hard to undo: a long contract, a senior hire, a change to a product people already love. Those deserve time, and I do not apologize for taking it. But most choices are not like that. A small test of a new label, a trial of a different process, a pilot with one customer can be reversed in a week at modest cost. Treating those with the gravity of the irreversible ones is how an organization slows to a crawl without anyone deciding it should.

This applies to hiring as well, which is the world I now work in at Provieo. People often treat every hiring step as equally weighty and so stretch the whole process across weeks. The candidate, meanwhile, is taking other calls. The strongest people tend to have options, and they read your pace as information about how you will run things once they arrive. A process that moves with care but without gaps says something good about the place. One that goes silent for ten days says something too, whatever the recruiter's email claims.

What a fast decision owes people

Speed alone is not the goal, and I have made fast calls that were simply bad. A quick decision carries obligations a slow one can skip. You owe the people affected a clear reason, even a short one. You owe them a stated moment when you will revisit it if it turns out wrong. And you owe them honesty when you reverse course, without dressing the reversal up as a plan. Teams forgive a wrong call made openly far more readily than they forgive a right one that arrived too late to use.

I do not think I have solved this for myself. I still feel the pull to wait, and some weeks I give in to it. What has changed is that I now count the cost of waiting as a real line item, the way I once counted the cost of capital. It does not appear on any statement, but it is paid, usually by the people who were most willing to do more and learned to stop offering. If you want to know whether your pace is a problem, do not look at your calendar. Look at how often your best people still bring you ideas you did not ask for.
