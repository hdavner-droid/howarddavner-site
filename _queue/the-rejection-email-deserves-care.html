The Rejection Email Deserves Care

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

I once kept a folder of rejections I had received early in my career, and I reread them years later out of curiosity. Nearly all of them said the same thing in slightly different words: after careful consideration, we have decided to move forward with other candidates, we wish you the best. I could not tell from any of them whether I had been close or nowhere near. I could not tell whether the decision took a week or a minute. What struck me, rereading them, was that I remembered almost nothing about those companies except how they had ended things. The two or three that said something real, even something mildly uncomfortable, were the ones I still thought well of.

I have been on the sending side often enough now to understand why the form letter exists. Rejecting someone is unpleasant, and the person doing it usually has a full calendar and a pile of other candidates still in motion. The template is a way of getting the task off the desk. I have used templates myself, and I have sometimes let a rejection sit for days because I did not know what to say and the silence felt easier than the sentence. I am not writing this from any high ground. I am writing it because I have come to think this small piece of correspondence is handled worse than almost anything else in hiring, and it costs more than we admit.

What the silence and the template actually say

A candidate who gets rejected has usually spent real hours on you. They researched the company, rearranged their week, prepared answers, and in many processes completed an assignment. When the reply is a boilerplate paragraph or nothing at all, the message underneath is that those hours were not worth a few minutes of yours. People may not complain about it, but they register it, and they tell the friend who asks whether they should apply there. For a company still small enough that its reputation travels by word of mouth, that is a quiet tax on every future search.

There is also a practical cost that is easy to miss. The people you reject are not a random sample of strangers. They are, by definition, people who were interested in what you do and qualified enough to get a conversation. Some of them will be right for a different role in a year. Some will become customers, or work at a company you later want to partner with, or be the person across the table in a negotiation. In the beverage business I have watched buyers, brokers, and former employees reappear in each other's orbits in ways nobody planned. Hiring is a small version of the same thing. The relationships do not end when the process does, and the ending shapes what comes after.

What a better rejection contains

I do not think the answer is long, elaborate feedback on every candidate. That can backfire, since a detailed critique invites an argument and there are real legal and practical reasons companies keep these messages short. But there is a wide gap between a paragraph of nothing and a full debrief. What I try to do now is include one specific thing. If someone's work sample showed unusual clarity in how they broke down a problem, I say that. If the deciding factor was that another candidate had direct experience with a situation we were about to walk into, I say that plainly, because it tells them the decision was about fit and timing rather than a verdict on their ability. One true, specific sentence does more than five polite ones.

I also try to be honest about what I do not know. Sometimes the choice between two finalists is close, and pretending it was clear makes the rejection feel manufactured. Saying it was a hard call, and that I could see the case for either of them, is more respectful than inventing a decisive reason. I have gotten this wrong in both directions. I have sent messages so cushioned that the candidate reasonably wondered whether they were still in the running, and I have sent messages that were blunter than they needed to be. The error toward vagueness is the more common one, and it is the one that wastes the other person's time, because they have to spend days deciding whether to follow up.

Speed matters more than polish

If I had to rank the parts of a rejection by how much they matter, timing would come first. A decision delivered promptly, even in plain language, lets a person redirect their energy. A beautifully written note that arrives three weeks late has already done its damage. People can accept a no. What wears on them is the ambiguity of waiting, checking an inbox, and trying to read meaning into a lack of reply. I now treat the rejection as part of the work of the search, not something that follows it, and I try to send it within a day or two of the decision being made, before the next stage of the process crowds it out.

This is part of why, at Provieo, we think so much about what candidates leave a process with. If hiring is supposed to rest on demonstrated work, then someone who has shown you real work and been turned down deserves to know what you saw in it. That is a better outcome than a form letter for them, and it is better for the company too, since it forces the people deciding to articulate their reasons instead of leaning on instinct. I am not certain every employer can or should do this at scale, and I am sure I still fall short of it on busy weeks. But I would rather hold myself to a standard of one honest sentence, sent quickly, than keep the folder of forgettable emails I once collected. The last message a candidate gets from you is not cleanup. It is part of the hire.
