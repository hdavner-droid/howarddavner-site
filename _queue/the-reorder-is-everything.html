The Reorder Is Everything

By Howard Davner · CEO, Beverage USA Holdings · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

The day you land a retail account feels like the finish line. Months of pitching, samples, follow-ups, and finally the yes — your product on the shelf of a store that matters. I've celebrated plenty of those days, and I've learned they're not finish lines at all. They're the start of a much quieter contest that nobody photographs: whether the product moves fast enough to earn the space it's occupying. In beverage, the shelf is rented, and velocity is the rent.

A buyer who says yes has given you a tryout, not a home. That slot is being measured against everything else that could sit there, and the measurement is unsentimental — units per store per week. If your product sits, it doesn't matter how good your story was or how much the buyer personally liked you. The space gets reclaimed by something that sells, and you're back to pitching. The first order is a loan against future velocity. The reorder is the only thing that says the loan is being repaid.

Distribution without velocity is a slow leak

Founders chase doors because doors are countable and impressive — "we're in a thousand stores" is a great line for a deck. But a thousand stores where the product doesn't move is not a business; it's a thousand small, quiet failures accumulating into a reputation with buyers you'll want again later. Retail is a small world with a long memory. It is genuinely better to be in a hundred stores where you fly off the shelf than a thousand where you gather dust, because the hundred compound and the thousand corrode.

The work starts after the shelf

Which means the real job begins the day after you win the account, and it's the least glamorous work there is: making sure people actually find and buy the thing now that it's there. Demos, the right shelf position, the second facing, local awareness, making the trip from "on the shelf" to "in the cart" as short as possible. This is unglamorous, grinding, store-by-store work, and it is the actual work. The pitch got you the tryout. Only velocity keeps the space.

So I've trained myself to feel muted about new doors and genuinely excited about reorders. A new account is a hypothesis. A reorder is evidence. When a store comes back and asks for more without being chased, the market has told you something true in the only language retail respects — and that single data point is worth more than a dozen first orders won on the strength of a good meeting. Anyone can get a yes once. The business is in the second yes.
