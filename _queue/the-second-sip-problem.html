The Second Sip Problem

By Howard Davner · CEO, Beverage USA Holdings · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

In functional beverages, everyone obsesses over the first sip. It's what the sample table is for, what the packaging promises, what the influencer films. And the first sip is genuinely important — if it's unpleasant, there's no second one. But the first sip is bought with novelty and marketing, and novelty is the one asset that depletes with use. The business isn't built on the first sip. It's built on the second one, and the two-hundredth, and almost nobody plans for those.

The second sip is where the story either holds or collapses. The consumer has swallowed the first one and is now, consciously or not, asking a quieter question: do I actually want more of this. Not "is this interesting" but "is this pleasant enough that reaching for it becomes a habit rather than a decision." A drink can be fascinating and still fail that test. Fascination gets you trial. It does not get you the case reorder that actually pays the bills.

Function and flavor are not a truce

The trap in our category is treating taste and function as a trade you negotiate — how much unpleasantness will the consumer tolerate in exchange for the benefit on the label. That framing quietly assumes the function is the point and the flavor is the tax. But nobody builds a daily habit around paying a tax. The drinks that last are the ones where you'd reach for them even if you forgot what they were supposed to do, because the second sip is simply good. The function is why they try it. The flavor is why they come back.

Design for the hundredth can

This changes how you formulate. A flavor engineered to dazzle at a sample table is often exhausting by the third can — too sweet, too loud, too much. The flavors that survive daily use tend to be the quieter ones, the ones that don't fatigue, that you could drink through a whole week without tiring of. That's a harder brief than "make it pop," and it's less rewarding in a taste test, which is exactly why so many brands optimize for the wrong moment and then wonder why trial never converts to loyalty.

I've come to believe the second sip is the most honest metric in our business, and one of the least measured. Trial numbers flatter everyone; they mostly measure your marketing. Reorders measure your product. When I look at whether something we make is going to work, I've stopped asking whether people are excited to try it and started asking whether they're quietly, unremarkably still drinking it a month later. The excitement fades on schedule. The habit is the whole company.
