The Shelf Talker Nobody Reads

By Howard Davner · Co-Founder, Beverage USA Holdings · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

Early on, I stood in a convenience store for about forty minutes and watched people walk past our cooler door. I wasn't there to sell anything. I just wanted to see what happened. A man in work boots looked at the shelf for what I'd guess was two seconds, reached past our can, and took something he'd clearly bought a hundred times. A woman picked ours up, turned it over, put it back, and picked up a sparkling water. Nobody read the paragraph we had paid a designer to fit on the back of the can. I had spent weeks on that paragraph, and I remember feeling a little foolish standing there with my arms crossed.

The lesson I took from that afternoon is not that copy doesn't matter. It is that most of what a brand writes is written for the people inside the brand. We write to reassure ourselves that the product is understood. We list the ingredients, explain the reasoning behind each one, and add a line about the mission. All of it is true, and almost none of it is read by a person standing at a cooler deciding in the time it takes to shift their weight from one foot to the other.

What a shopper actually does in two seconds

When I think about what a shopper takes in, I now think in layers. The first layer is shape and color, and it works before any language does. The shopper is sorting the shelf into familiar and unfamiliar, and into things that look like they are for them and things that do not. The second layer is a handful of words, maybe three or four, and they are read only if the first layer earned a second look. The third layer is the back of the package, which a small fraction of people reach, and those people are usually already leaning toward buying. By the time someone reads the back, the decision is mostly made, and the text is there to give them permission.

That reframed how I thought about the front of the can. The question stopped being what we want to say and became what a stranger could possibly learn in a glance. In our case the honest answer was less than we hoped. We could communicate roughly one idea, and only if we chose it ruthlessly. I will admit we did not choose well the first time. We put three ideas on the front because no one on the team could agree on which to drop, and the result was a can that said a little about everything and nothing memorably about anything.

The words that work are borrowed

What I noticed in stores was that the words people respond to are mostly ones they already own. A shopper does not want to learn a new vocabulary for a drink. They want to recognize something: a flavor they know, an occasion they have, a problem they had at three in the afternoon last Tuesday. The best phrases on a package are not clever. They are phrases a customer could have said themselves. When we finally rewrote the front around a plain description of when someone would reach for the drink, the can got easier to explain, and our sales staff noticed before any number did. They stopped needing to translate it.

The shelf talker, the little printed card that hangs off the edge of a shelf, is the clearest case of this. They tend to be written like miniature advertisements, with a slogan and an adjective or two. In the stores I watched, they were close to invisible, partly because shoppers have learned to treat them as noise and partly because they say what the brand wants rather than what the shopper is wondering. The few that seemed to work answered a single practical question, such as what this tastes like or how it compares with the thing next to it. I cannot prove that from any controlled test, only from standing and watching, so I hold the conclusion loosely.

Writing for the person who is not there

The deeper discipline here is writing for someone who is distracted, a little tired, and not especially interested in you. That is the real customer for most products, and it is uncomfortable to accept, because the people who build a product are the opposite of that. We are obsessed. We forget how little attention the world has spare. Every time I have tried to improve our packaging by adding explanation, I have made it worse, and every time I have improved it by removing something, a colleague has asked me whether we were sure. I have started to treat that question as a sign I am on the right track.

I still think the paragraph on the back of the can has a job. It serves the curious person, the skeptic, and the buyer who wants to check a claim before trusting it, and that audience is smaller but loyal. What I got wrong was believing it could carry the sale. A package has to win in two seconds without help, and only then does the rest of the writing get a chance to matter. These days when I review a design, I hold it at arm's length, glance, and look away. Then I ask what I remember. If the answer is nothing, we are not finished, no matter how good the paragraph is.
