What a Free Sample Really Costs

By Howard Davner · CEO, Beverage USA Holdings · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

Early on, I stood behind a demo table in a grocery store and watched a woman take a small cup of our drink, sip it, wince slightly, and set it down on a shelf of canned beans. She was polite about it. She said thank you and kept walking. I remember that moment better than most of the sales we made that day, because I realized I had just paid for a stranger to form a negative opinion of something I had spent years on. Nobody warns you that a free sample can cost you a customer.

Most companies file sampling under marketing spend, and the logic is simple: put product in mouths, some of those mouths become buyers. That is true as far as it goes. But it treats the sample as a giveaway when it is really an experiment. Every cup handed over is a small test of your product, your pitch, and your assumptions about who wants this. If you are only counting cups, you are throwing away most of what the table can teach you.

The wrong crowd

My first mistake was choosing the busiest locations and the busiest hours. Traffic felt like the right metric. What I got was a crowd that included a lot of people who take a sample because it is free, not because they are curious about the category. Some came back for a third cup. They were cheerful and complimentary, and they almost never bought anything. I logged that as a good day because the table was never empty. I now think it was mostly noise, and it made the product look better than it was.

The people who taught me the most were quieter. They picked up the can and read the back. They asked what was in it, or how much caffeine, or whether it would keep them up. Those are the questions of someone who might actually buy, and they are also the questions that tell you whether your label and your explanation are working. A demo table full of enthusiastic strangers tells you very little. A demo table where three people in a row ask the same question tells you something about your packaging.

There is a harder point underneath this. A functional drink is a product where the taste is only the first gate. The thing we actually promise is an effect over the following hours, and nobody can verify that standing in an aisle. So a sample delivers a partial product. If the flavor is merely fine, we have handed someone a partial experience and asked them to judge the whole. I do not have a clean answer to that problem. I only know that it makes sampling riskier for us than it is for a soda or a chip, and that we should be more selective about whom we ask.

Sample where the product lives

The second lesson was about logistics, and it was embarrassingly simple. A sample without available product nearby is a wasted sample. Early on, we ran a demo on a day when the product was not yet on the cooler shelf, or was in the wrong spot, or was sold out by midafternoon. Someone liked the taste, wanted a can, and could not find one. Whatever intent we had created evaporated by the time they reached the parking lot. Now I will not approve a demo unless I have confirmed the product is in the cooler that day and that someone can point to where it is.

Placement of the table matters for the same reason. A table at the front of the store meets people while they are still deciding what their trip is about. A table near the cooler meets them when the purchase is one step away. We saw far better conversion from the second setup, though I want to be honest that I am relying on impressions and store feedback here, not a controlled comparison. Retail data rarely lets you isolate one variable, and I distrust anyone who claims it neatly does.

What I ask for now

These days I care less about how many cups went out and more about a few plainer things. Did the person finish the sample, or set it down half full? What did they ask? Did the person running the table feel comfortable saying, this may not be for you, when someone told them they avoid caffeine? That last one matters more than I expected. The best demo staff we have worked with were not the most persuasive. They were the most honest, and shoppers tend to notice.

I still believe in sampling. Nothing replaces the moment a person tastes the product and decides for themselves. But I have stopped thinking of it as free, and I have stopped thinking of volume as the goal. Each sample is a chance to earn a customer or lose one, and the cost of getting it wrong is paid in opinions that are very hard to change. I would rather hand out fewer cups to the right people, in the right place, with product waiting on the shelf, than fill a table with people who will never come back for a second can.
