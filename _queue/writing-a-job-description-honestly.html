Writing a Job Description Honestly

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · {{MONTH_YEAR}}

Some time ago I reread a posting I had written for an operations role and tried to picture the person it described. It asked for several years of distribution experience, fluency with spreadsheets, a self-starter attitude, and a willingness to wear many hats. I could not have told you what that person would be doing on a given Tuesday. I had written the posting in twenty minutes by adapting an older one, which had itself been adapted from something else. Nobody, including me, could have said whether a candidate had met the standard, because the standard was a mood.

I think this is how most job descriptions get made. They are assembled from old postings, from what a competitor seems to be asking for, and from the anxieties of whoever has to approve them. Each person adds a requirement so that the hire will not disappoint them in some particular way, and no one removes anything. The result describes a person rather than a problem. It is a wish list, and candidates know it, so they respond in kind. They match the language, mirror the keywords, and send a resume shaped like the posting. That was already a weak filter, and it is weaker now that anyone can generate a perfectly matched application in a minute.

Describe the problem, not the person

The change that helped me most was to stop listing traits and start describing a situation. Instead of “manage distributor relationships,” I wrote something closer to this: a couple of our distributors reorder later than they should, we do not fully understand why, and we need someone to find out and fix it. That sentence does more work than a paragraph of qualifications. It tells a candidate what the job feels like, and it gives them something to react to besides a checklist.

The responses changed in ways I had not predicted. Some people still sent the standard application. But a few wrote back with questions. They asked how we tracked reorders, whether the late ones were small accounts or large ones, and whether anyone had called the buyers directly. Those were the people I most wanted to talk to, and under the old posting I would never have seen that instinct, because there was nothing in the posting to ask about. A good question about a real problem tells you more than a confident answer about a generic one.

I should say that I did not get this right the first time. My early rewrites were so specific that they probably scared off people who could have learned the work quickly. And in at least one case, trying to write the problem down showed me that I did not actually know what it was. I knew I was busy and wanted help, which is not the same thing. That was uncomfortable, but it was useful. A posting you cannot write honestly is often a hire you have not yet thought through.

What honest postings filter for

Once you describe the real work, you also have to describe the unattractive parts. The budget is tight. Some processes are undefined. A fair amount of the job is repetitive follow-up that nobody will notice. I resisted putting this in writing, because it seemed like it would shrink the pool. In my experience it does shrink the pool, and the people who leave are mostly the ones who would have been unhappy in month three. I cannot prove that with data, and I would be wary of anyone who claimed to. It is what I have seen.

My finance background probably shaped this. A good investment memo has a section on what could go wrong, and readers trust the memo more for it. A document that only sells makes you suspect what it leaves out. A job posting works the same way. When it names the hard parts, the candidate has a reason to believe the rest of it, and the conversation that follows starts from something closer to the truth.

This is also where my thinking about Provieo comes from. We built it around the idea that people should be hired on the strength of work they have actually done, and that is hard to act on if the employer has only listed credentials. A list of credentials invites credentials in return. A described problem invites work. Someone can respond with a short analysis, a draft plan, or a worked example of how they would approach the thing in front of them. The posting and the evidence can finally speak to each other.

A test before you post

I now use a simple check before anything goes live. I hand the draft to someone on the team who will work with the hire and ask them what this person will be doing on their first Tuesday. If they can answer without guessing, the posting is ready. If they hesitate, or if their answer differs from mine, we are not ready to hire yet, and no amount of polish will fix that. I do not claim this catches everything. I have still written postings that were vaguer than I realized. But it has saved me from the worst version of the mistake, which is spending weeks searching for a person I had never actually described.
