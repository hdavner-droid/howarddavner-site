Engineering the Energy Curve: Howard Davner on Building a Focus Drink That Doesn't Crash

By Howard Davner · CEO, Beverage USA Holdings · July 2026

The most common question I get about functional energy drinks is why some leave you wired and then wrecked an hour later, while others carry you evenly through an afternoon. I'm Howard Davner, CEO of Beverage USA Holdings and co-founder of NERD Focus, and the honest answer is that the difference isn't luck or a secret ingredient. It's the shape of the energy curve — and that shape is something you design on purpose, one formulation decision at a time.

What a crash actually is

A crash isn't a mystery. It's a curve problem. When a drink loads you with a big hit of caffeine and a wave of sugar, you climb fast — blood sugar spikes, stimulants flood in, and for twenty minutes you feel unstoppable. Then the sugar clears, insulin overcorrects, and the adenosine your caffeine was blocking comes rushing back all at once. The peak was too tall, so the fall is too steep. The drink didn't give you energy so much as borrow it from the next hour and charge interest. Most people blame themselves for the slump. Usually they should blame the formula.

Designing for the plateau, not the peak

When we build a focus product, the goal is a plateau, not a spike. That means a moderate, deliberate caffeine dose instead of the largest number the label can carry. It means pairing caffeine with L-theanine, an amino acid that smooths the edge off stimulation and supports calm attention rather than jitter. It means refusing the sugar bomb and leaning on B vitamins and clean ingredients that support sustained output. Individually these look like small choices. Together they decide whether someone feels sharp for three hours or electric for thirty minutes and foggy after.

The formulation is a series of honest trade-offs

Here's the tension I've learned to sit with: more caffeine almost always tests better in a single sip and worse over two hours. A sweeter, punchier drink wins the first taste test and loses the repeat. It takes real discipline to build for the second experience instead of the first, because the first is the one focus groups reward. But the customer doesn't live in the focus group. They live in the actual afternoon, at their desk or on the road, and what they remember is how they felt at hour two. Designing for that moment means leaving some of the easy, showy intensity on the table.

Why the curve is a business decision, too

This is where product science and company-building meet. A crash is a broken promise, and broken promises don't get repurchased. In a category where the whole business runs on someone reaching for your can again next week, the energy curve is not a nutrition-panel footnote — it's the product. I'd rather make a drink that feels a little less explosive up front and leaves people clear-headed and loyal than one that dazzles once and disappoints by 3 p.m. Getting the curve right is slower, quieter work than marketing a big caffeine number. It's also the only version that compounds. That, more than any single ingredient, is what I mean when I say a functional beverage is really a promise you keep across time.
