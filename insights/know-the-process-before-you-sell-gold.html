Know the Process Before You Sell Your Gold

By Howard Davner · Touchstone Gold and Silver · November 2026

Most people who sell gold do it once or twice in their lives. They bring in a broken chain, a single earring, a ring from a marriage that ended, or a box from a parent's dresser. They are not experts, and they shouldn't have to be. But the gold-buying business has long depended on the gap between what the buyer knows and what the seller knows. At Touchstone Gold and Silver, I, Howard Davner, decided early that the gap is the problem we should close, not the advantage we should keep.

So every one of our buying sessions starts the same way: before anyone buys anything, we teach the room how the process works.

Why education comes first

A seller who understands the process asks better questions, trusts the answer, and walks out feeling respected whether or not they sell. A seller who doesn't understand it is left with one number and a decision to make under pressure. I spent more than a decade in finance, and the lesson carries over directly: when one side of a transaction can't see how the price was built, the transaction isn't really fair, even if nobody lied. Explaining the method up front is the simplest way I know to make a sale fair.

What a seller should understand before selling

1. How the item is tested. Gold jewelry is marked with a karat stamp, but stamps can be worn, wrong, or missing, and plated pieces can look identical to solid gold. A reputable buyer tests every piece and does it where you can see it, so you watch the result rather than being told it.

2. How it is weighed. Gold is valued by weight, so the scale matters. It should be a certified scale, and you should be able to read the display yourself. Know which unit is being used: precious metals are usually weighed in grams or pennyweight, not in the ounces on your kitchen scale.

3. What actually sets the value. Three things drive the price of scrap gold: its purity (10, 14, 18 or 22 karat contain very different amounts of gold), its weight, and that day's metal price. A buyer's offer is a share of the metal value, because the gold still has to be refined. You are entitled to know how the number was reached.

4. What the law requires. Legitimate buyers ask for photo ID and record each purchase, because state and local secondhand and precious-metal laws require it. That is a sign you are dealing with a business that follows the rules. A buyer who doesn't care who you are should worry you.

5. That you can say no. An offer is an offer. You can take it, think about it, or leave with everything you brought.

Red flags

If a buyer won't test in front of you, won't let you see the scale, can't explain how they got to their number, or presses you to decide right now, don't sell. The same goes for anyone asking you to mail gold to a stranger or hand it to a courier. Good buyers have nothing to hide in the process, so they show it.

Why this is good business, too

Some people assume that teaching sellers costs the buyer money. In my experience it does the opposite. People tell friends and family when they were treated fairly, and many of our sellers come back, or send someone else, because they understood what happened the first time. Transparency is slower for the first ten minutes of a session and faster for everything after. The same principle runs through every business I'm part of, from Provieo to Beverage USA Holdings: show your work, and trust follows.

The takeaway

Before you sell gold anywhere, ask the buyer to walk you through testing, weighing and pricing. A good one will be glad you asked. If they aren't, you have your answer.
