The Portfolio Is the New Résumé

By Howard Davner · Founder, Provieo · August 2026

The résumé had a good run. For most of the last century it was a reasonable compression of a person: where they studied, where they worked, what they were trusted to do. It was never a great instrument, but it was cheap to read and it correlated, loosely, with ability. That correlation is breaking, and it's breaking fast.

The reason is simple. A document that anyone can now generate to look perfect no longer carries information. When every applicant arrives with flawless phrasing, the right keywords, and an achievement list tuned to the posting, the résumé stops telling the employer anything that distinguishes one person from another. It has become a formality that everyone passes — which means it decides nothing.

What replaces it is evidence

When claims are free, proof becomes valuable. The thing that can't be faked at scale is a body of real work aimed at the job you actually want: the analysis you ran, the thing you built, the problem you took from messy to solved, presented in a way that lets someone see how you think. That's a portfolio. Designers and developers have understood this for years; what's changed is that it's now becoming true for nearly everyone — in marketing, operations, finance, analysis, strategy.

A portfolio does something a résumé structurally cannot. A résumé tells; a portfolio shows. One asks the reader to trust a summary; the other hands them the artifact and says, judge for yourself. In a market drowning in confident summaries, "judge for yourself" is a powerful offer.

"Job-tailored" is the part people miss

A generic portfolio isn't much better than a generic résumé. The version that works is tailored to the specific role: if you want the job, build the thing that job actually requires and show it to the person doing the hiring. Want to work in growth marketing? Don't describe your interest — run a real teardown of the company's funnel and propose three experiments. Want the analyst seat? Model the thing they'd hand you in week one. It's more work than firing off a hundred applications, and that's exactly why it cuts through. Effort aimed at a specific target is itself a signal.

This is the idea I built Provieo around. Most people are more capable than their résumé lets them prove, and most of the hiring market is starved for a real signal it can trust. The fix is to help people produce demonstrable, role-specific work — and to make that work legible to the people who decide. Not a better way to describe yourself. A way to stop describing and start showing.

Where this goes

I don't think the résumé disappears overnight; institutions are sticky, and a lot of hiring pipelines are still built around it. But its weight is falling, and the weight of demonstrated work is rising, and that trend only accelerates as generated text gets cheaper and more perfect. The winners in the next decade of hiring — on both sides of the table — will be the ones who figured out early that the question is no longer "what does your résumé say." It's "what can you show me you've actually done."
