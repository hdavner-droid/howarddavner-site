What Your Old Pokémon Cards Are Really Worth

By Howard Davner · Touchstone Gold and Silver · November 2026

A binder of Pokémon cards is one of the most common things families bring to a buying session now. Sometimes it belongs to a grown child who collected in the late 1990s; sometimes it's a teenager's current collection. Either way, the first question is the same: is any of this worth real money? I'm Howard Davner, and at Touchstone Gold and Silver we buy Pokémon cards from collectors and families. Here is how I'd think about a collection before selling it.

The headlines are real, and they are rare

In February 2026, a PSA 10 Pikachu Illustrator sold at Goldin for $16,492,000 including buyer's premium, which Guinness World Records verified as the most expensive trading card ever sold at auction. That card was a 1998 contest prize, not something anyone pulled from a pack. Further down, the top Pokémon sales of 2025 included a first-edition Charizard in PSA 10 at $550,000 and a 1996 Japanese "no rarity symbol" Charizard in PSA 10 at about $641,700.

Those numbers tell you what the very top of the hobby looks like. They don't tell you what an ordinary Charizard in a binder sleeve is worth. Almost every famous sale shares two things: a scarce card, and a near-perfect grade. Take away either one and the value drops, often by a lot.

What actually drives value

1. Which printing it is. Early English cards from the original Base Set era, especially those marked "1st Edition," are worth far more than later printings of the same artwork. Japanese promos and early Japanese releases have their own following. Check the small symbols and text on the card, not just the character.

2. Rarity within the set. Holographic cards, and in modern sets the special illustration and alternate-art cards, carry most of a collection's value. Common cards from recent sets are usually worth very little each, even in perfect condition, because so many were printed.

3. Condition. This is where most of the money is won or lost. Look at the four corners, the edges (especially the back, where whitening shows), the surface for scratches on the holo, and the centering of the borders. A card that was played with, shuffled or kept loose in a shoebox will almost never grade at the top.

4. Demand for the character. Charizard and Pikachu drive a large share of the market. A popular character in great condition will usually outsell an obscure one from the same set.

Grading: when it's worth it

Third-party graders such as PSA, Beckett (BGS) and CGC seal a card in a case with a numeric grade and a certification number. Pokémon is now the biggest part of that business. According to GemRate data reported by Sports Illustrated, PSA graded nearly 20 million items in 2025, and more than 11 million of them were trading-card-game cards, mostly Pokémon.

That demand has consequences for sellers. PSA reported a backlog of roughly 12 million cards at the end of June 2026 and paused its lowest-priced "value" tiers starting June 2 until the queue came down. Grading costs money and takes time, so it only makes sense for cards whose value clearly justifies it. A good buyer will tell you honestly which of your cards, if any, are worth sending in before you decide anything.

How to spot fakes

Counterfeits are a real problem. In its first fraud report, covered by USA Today in May 2026, PSA said it identified more than $200 million worth of counterfeit and altered trading cards in 2025, and that six of the ten most-counterfeited cards were Pokémon. A few checks anyone can do at home:

  • The light test. Genuine cards have a dark inner layer, so very little light passes through when you hold a flashlight behind them.
  • Feel and finish. Fakes often feel too thin, too stiff or too glossy. Textured modern cards should have raised texture you can feel.
  • Fonts and symbols. Compare the HP number, attack text, energy symbols and copyright line against a card you know is real. Wrong fonts and misspellings are common giveaways.
  • The back. The blue swirl on a real card is smooth; fakes are often too dark, washed out or blotchy.
  • Graded cases. Fake slabs exist too. Look up the certification number on the grading company's website and make sure the card and grade match.

Be careful with any home test that risks damage, such as bending or tearing. Never do that to a card you think might be real.

How to sell a collection without being shortchanged

  • Don't clean, trim or remove cards from cases. Well-meant cleanup can lower the grade a card could have earned.
  • Sort before you sell. Pull holos, 1st Edition cards, Japanese promos, alternate-art cards and anything in a graded case into a separate pile. The rest can usually be valued as a lot.
  • Ask how each number was reached. A fair buyer prices the important cards one at a time against recent sales of the same card in similar condition, and can show you the comparison.
  • Get one written offer, with no obligation. You should be able to take it home and think about it. Photo ID is recorded as the law requires; that's normal.
  • Know the alternatives. For a genuinely rare card in top condition, a specialist auction may bring more, with fees and a wait. For a mixed binder, a direct sale is faster and simpler. Either can be the right answer.

Many of the same principles apply to sports cards and comic books. And it's the same rule I apply to gold: the seller should understand the process before anything is bought. An informed seller makes a better decision, whatever they decide.
