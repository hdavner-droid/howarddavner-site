What Your Old Sports Cards Are Really Worth

By Howard Davner · Touchstone Gold and Silver · November 2026

Shoeboxes of baseball cards are one of the most common things families bring to us, right behind jewelry and coins. Most people have heard the stories of cards selling for millions, and most have also heard that cards from their childhood are "worthless." Both are true for some cards. I'm Howard Davner, and at Touchstone Gold and Silver we buy sports cards from collectors and families. Here is how to tell which kind you have.

The hobby is busy, but it's split in two

Card trading is at a high level of activity. Market tracker Card Ladder reported that October 2026 was the first month ever with more than $1 billion in trading card sales, and Heritage Auctions' sports director has said prices are at or beyond the peak of the 2021 pandemic boom. But that money is not spread evenly. Dealers describe a market running in two different realities: scarce, high-grade cards of established stars keep drawing strong bids, while mass-produced modern cards and speculative rookies have cooled. Knowing which side of that line your cards sit on is most of the battle.

Start with the year

Before about 1980 (vintage). Older cards were printed in smaller numbers and were handled, flipped and thrown away by the kids who owned them, so few survive in good shape. Stars and Hall of Famers from these years, and especially their rookie cards, are where real value tends to be. Even well-worn vintage cards of major players can be worth more than people expect.

Roughly 1987 to 1993 (the "junk wax" era). Card companies printed cards by the billions in these years, and collectors saved everything. Supply never ran out, so the vast majority of these cards sell for very little, often just pennies apiece. There are exceptions: the 1989 Upper Deck Ken Griffey Jr. rookie, the 1993 SP Derek Jeter rookie and the 1990 Topps Frank Thomas "No Name" error are the famous ones, along with Michael Jordan's 1986 Fleer rookie just before the era. Even these need excellent condition to bring serious money.

Mid-1990s to today. Manufacturers responded to overproduction with inserts, parallels, autographs and serial-numbered cards. A base card from this period is usually common, but a low-numbered parallel or an autograph of a star can be valuable. These cards need to be looked at one by one.

Condition and grading

Collectors grade cards on a ten-point scale, looking at corners, edges, surface and how well the picture is centered. At the top of the scale small differences mean big money, and a PSA 10 of a popular card can sell for many times a PSA 8. Companies such as PSA, SGC and BGS seal a card in a case with its grade, which makes it much easier to sell.

Grading has also become more expensive and slower. PSA raised prices on its lower tiers in 2026, with its cheapest bulk option at $24.99 per card, and opened a $59.99 Standard tier in September. SGC's lowest rate rose to $50. Waits at the major graders can run weeks to months. The math is simple: grading pays only when a card is likely to be worth well more than the fee in high grade. A good buyer will tell you honestly which of your cards, if any, are worth grading.

How to sell a collection without being shortchanged

  • Don't clean, trim or "fix" anything. Altered cards lose value, and graders can detect it.
  • Handle cards by the edges and keep them sleeved. A fresh crease can erase most of a card's value.
  • Sort out the obvious stars and older cards first. Hall of Famers, rookie cards and anything before 1980 deserve a closer look. Junk wax commons can be priced as a lot.
  • Ask how each offer was reached. A fair buyer prices key cards against recent sales of the same card in similar condition and can show you the comparison.
  • Get it in writing, with no pressure. You should be able to take an offer home and think it over.

It's the same principle I apply to gold and to comic books: the seller should understand the process before anything is bought. An informed seller makes a better decision, whatever they decide.
